## What changed from v1

v1 gave fifteen drafts and a structural observation. v2 adds the evidence base, and the evidence base **changed the design in three ways** rather than merely decorating it:

1. **Internal attentional focus impairs motor performance.** That is well established, and it means a grounding moment during a heavy or technical lift is not neutral — it may make the lift worse. v1's G9 and G10 did exactly that. Corrected below.
2. **Interoceptive attention is not safe for everyone.** For a product built for burnout, trauma and chronic conditions, this is a first-order design constraint, not a footnote. v1 had nothing on it.
3. **Compassion is trainable; empathy alone is not protective.** This sharpens what the *Beyond* step should ask for, and explains why v1's G7 read as overreach.

Drafts are rewritten throughout. Three from v1 are cut.

# Part 1 — The evidence

> **Citation status.** These are given in good faith from the literature and are, to my knowledge, accurate in author, year, journal and finding. **Every one should be checked against the source before it appears in anything public-facing** — a website, a funding application, or a claim to a partner organisation. Inside this document they are working references, not published claims. Where I am less certain I have said so.

## 1.1 Attention during movement — and the tension nobody mentions

**Internal focus** means attending to your own body: the contact of your forearms, the muscle doing the work. **External focus** means attending to the effect or the environment: the floor, the horizon, the implement.

**Wulf, G. (2013). Attentional focus and motor learning: a review of 15 years. *International Review of Sport and Exercise Psychology*, 6(1), 77–104.**
The consolidated finding across a very large literature: an **external** focus produces better movement efficiency, accuracy, force production and retention than an internal one. This is one of the more robust effects in motor learning.

**Calatayud, J. et al. (2016). Importance of mind-muscle connection during progressive resistance training. *European Journal of Applied Physiology*, 116(3), 527–533.**
Yet internal focus reliably *increases EMG activation* in the targeted muscle.

**Schoenfeld, B. et al. (2018). Differential effects of attentional focus strategies during long-term resistance training. *European Journal of Sport Science*, 18(5), 705–712.**
Over eight weeks, an internal focus produced greater biceps hypertrophy than an external one; leg outcomes did not differ significantly.

**So the honest position is a trade-off, and it cuts against the intuition in the plank example.** Internal attention may build more muscle in isolation work. It also tends to make you move worse. For a barbell squat, a heavy deadlift, or anything with a technical failure mode, that trade is not worth taking — and the product should not ask someone to take it mid-lift.

**Design consequence.** Grounding moments belong in movements where performance is not the point and failure is not sharp: **isometric holds, steady-state cardio, mobility, floor work, seated work, walking**. They do **not** belong in the working set of a heavy or technical lift. v1 put them there. That was wrong, and it came from taking the plank example's mechanism and generalising it further than the evidence goes.

They may sit **between** sets. Not during one.

## 1.2 Why nature, specifically

Graeme's plank example reaches for a field, and his running example for birds and wind. That instinct is well supported.

**Kaplan, S. (1995). The restorative benefits of nature: toward an integrative framework. *Journal of Environmental Psychology*, 15(3), 169–182.**
Attention Restoration Theory. Directed attention is a depletable resource; natural environments engage "soft fascination", which holds attention without effortful direction, allowing the directed-attention system to recover. **This is the mechanism that makes a grounding moment restorative rather than merely pleasant.**

**Ulrich, R. et al. (1991). Stress recovery during exposure to natural and urban environments. *Journal of Environmental Psychology*, 11(3), 201–230.**
Stress Recovery Theory. Natural scenes produce faster physiological recovery from stress than urban ones.

**Bratman, G. et al. (2015). Nature experience reduces rumination and subgenual prefrontal cortex activation. *PNAS*, 112(28), 8567–8572.**
A 90-minute nature walk reduced self-reported rumination and activity in a region associated with it; a matched urban walk did not. Directly relevant to a product whose users include people in burnout.

**Barton, J. & Pretty, J. (2010). What is the best dose of nature and green exercise for improving mental health? *Environmental Science & Technology*, 44(10), 3947–3955.**
Dose–response across UK studies: the largest gains in self-esteem and mood come from **short** exposures. Effect present at five minutes.

**Design consequence — and it is the strongest argument in this document.** If the benefit is front-loaded at around five minutes, then a *ninety-second* grounding moment is not a diluted version of a nature walk. It sits inside the window where the effect is steepest. **Brevity is the design, not a compromise.**

It also licenses the imagined field in the plank. Which brings the obvious objection:

**Van Hedger, S. et al. (2019)** and related work on imagined and simulated nature suggest that imagery and natural sound produce attenuated but real restorative effects relative to actual nature exposure. *I am moderately confident of this direction of finding and less certain of the specific citation — verify before relying on it.* The safe claim: imagining a meadow is weaker than standing in one, and better than nothing.

## 1.3 Interoception — the mechanism of the *Contact* step

**Craig, A. D. (2009). How do you feel — now? The anterior insula and human awareness. *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*, 10(1), 59–70.**
Interoception — the sense of the body's internal state — is integrated in the anterior insula and underlies subjective feeling states.

**Farb, N. et al. (2015). Interoception, contemplative practice, and health. *Frontiers in Psychology*, 6, 763.**
Reviews how contemplative training alters interoceptive attention, and distinguishes **helpful** interoceptive awareness from **unhelpful** interoceptive vigilance — attending to the body with curiosity versus scanning it for threat.

**Mehling, W. et al. (2012). The Multidimensional Assessment of Interoceptive Awareness (MAIA). *PLoS ONE*, 7(11), e48230.**
Separates adaptive dimensions (noticing, attention regulation, trusting) from maladaptive ones (worrying, not-distracting).

**Design consequence.** The *Contact* step is interoceptive training in miniature, and the line between "notice where your forearms meet the ground" and "scan your body for what is wrong" is one of tone, not content. The copy must invite curiosity and never diagnosis. **No grounding moment should ever ask whether something hurts.** Pain has its own place in this product; it must not colonise this one.

## 1.4 The safety constraint v1 missed entirely

**Britton, W. (2019). Can mindfulness be too much of a good thing? *Current Opinion in Psychology*, 28, 159–165.**
Meditative and body-focused practices have a dose-response curve with a descending limb. Adverse effects are under-reported, not absent.

**Farias, M. et al. (2020). Adverse events in meditation practices and meditation-based therapies: a systematic review. *Acta Psychiatrica Scandinavica*, 142(5), 374–393.**
Meta-analytic prevalence of adverse events around 8%, comparable to other psychological interventions. Most common: anxiety and depression.

**Heide, F. & Borkovec, T. (1983). Relaxation-induced anxiety. *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology*, 51(2), 171–182.**
For some people, instructed relaxation increases anxiety rather than lowering it. Established for decades and routinely forgotten.

**Design consequences, all three of which are requirements rather than preferences:**

- **Attention must be directable outward, always.** Every family below needs at least one *Place* or *Beyond* moment, so nobody is ever offered only body-focused attention. Someone for whom interoception is destabilising must still get something.
- **Dismissable without penalty, and the dismissal remembered.** No "are you sure", no re-offering the same one, nothing logged as a skip. Consistent with the existing no-shame architecture and with `empathyPromptSkips` widening the gap rather than nagging.
- **Never during the app's crisis or severe-pain paths.** Someone routed to the Gentle Care card is not a candidate for "notice where your body meets the floor."

**This is also why these must stay free.** Safety-critical behaviour is never paywalled, and the opt-out is part of the feature, not a lesser version of it.

## 1.5 The *Beyond* step — what the evidence actually supports

**Batson, C. D. (1987). Prosocial motivation: is it ever truly altruistic? *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology*, 20, 65–122.** *(already in the project)*
Empathic concern for another produces genuinely altruistic motivation, distinct from egoistic relief of one's own distress.

**Decety, J. & Jackson, P. (2004). The functional architecture of human empathy. *Behavioral and Cognitive Neuroscience Reviews*, 3(2), 71–100.** *(Decety 2011 already in the project)*
Empathy is componential: affective sharing, self–other distinction, and perspective-taking. **They can be trained separately.**

**Klimecki, O. et al. (2013). Functional neural plasticity and associated changes in positive affect after compassion training. *Cerebral Cortex*, 23(7), 1552–1561.**
The critical result. **Empathy training alone increased negative affect and empathic distress. Compassion training increased positive affect and produced a different neural signature.** Feeling *with* someone is costly; wishing them well is not.

**Weng, H. et al. (2013). Compassion training alters altruism and neural responses to suffering. *Psychological Science*, 24(7), 1171–1180.**
Two weeks of brief compassion training increased altruistic behaviour and altered neural responses to suffering. **Brief practice was sufficient.**

**Neff, K. (2003). Self-compassion: an alternative conceptualization of a healthy attitude toward oneself. *Self and Identity*, 2(2), 85–101.**
Self-compassion predicts wellbeing without the fragility of self-esteem, which depends on favourable comparison. Directly relevant to a product that refuses comparison on principle.

**Design consequences.**

- **Ask for well-wishing, not for feeling-with.** *"See what happens if you assume it was a hard one"* is perspective-taking with warmth. *"Imagine how bad they must feel"* is empathic distress. v1's G7 sat closer to the second than I realised when I wrote it. **This is the single most important correction in v2.**
- **Weng supports the format.** Brief, repeated compassion practice is enough. Ninety seconds inside a plank is a plausible dose, not wishful thinking.
- **Self-directed and other-directed both belong.** Neff's work says the *Beyond* step need not always point outward — pointing it at yourself is a legitimate version, not a shallower one.

## 1.6 Burnout

**Plumbly, C. (2024). *The Trauma Sponge*.** *(already in the project)*
Informs the coaching architecture throughout. The relevant principle: a depleted person needs restoration offered without demand attached. A grounding moment must never read as one more thing to do well.

# Part 2 — The design

**Contact → Place → Beyond.** Body, then world, then other people. Derived from Graeme's two examples; supported by §1.3, §1.2 and §1.5 respectively.

**These map onto the Empathy Transfer stages.** *Contact* is stage 1 self-recognition; *Beyond* is stage 3 situational empathy. The running example ends on *"wondering what their day might have been like"*, which is a stage 3 prompt almost verbatim. **Grounding moments are Empathy Transfer's practice ground** — and the reason it might land for someone who never reaches session 85.

## Depth, not just variety

Per §1.5, *Beyond* asks more of a person than *Contact* does. Offered in week one it reads as instruction; in month four, as invitation.

**Proposal:** depth gated on sessions completed, using the same shape as `STAGE_SESSION_FLOOR`.

| Depth | Available from | Asks |
|---|---|---|
| Contact | Session 1 | Notice a physical sensation |
| Place | ~Session 8 | Notice or imagine surroundings |
| Beyond | ~Session 20 | Hold another person in mind |

Cheap, because that machinery now exists.

## Where they may and may not appear

| Movement | Grounding moment? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Isometric holds | ✅ During | The plank model. No technical failure mode |
| Steady-state cardio, walking | ✅ During | The running model. §1.2 |
| Mobility, floor work, seated | ✅ During | Low performance stakes |
| Yoga | ✅ During | Already the frame |
| **Heavy or technical lifts** | ⚠️ **Between sets only** | **§1.1 — internal focus impairs performance** |
| Balance work | ⚠️ Between only | Attention shift while balancing is a fall risk |
| Crisis path, severe pain, Gentle Care | ❌ **Never** | §1.4 |
| Breathing, quiet sessions | ❌ Never | Already grounding. Adding another layer is noise |

## Frequency

Roughly one in three or four sessions, never twice running, never in a person's first session. These depend on being slightly unexpected; at every session they become furniture. Empathy transfer's four-session gap is the working model.

# Part 3 — The moments

Rewritten. Voice: second person, short, invitational. No exclamation, no praise, no question the person must answer, nothing measured afterwards.

## Isometric holds — plank, wall sit, dead hang, side plank

**G1 · Contact**
> Before the shake starts — where are you actually touching the ground? Forearms, elbows, the heel of your hands. Find the spot taking the most. That one point is doing a lot of quiet work.

**G2 · Place**
> If you were holding this in a field, what would be under your hands? See if you can put grass there. Cool, slightly damp, giving a little.

**G3 · Beyond**
> You are a line held above the ground, and everything around you is growing. Grass, flowers, whatever is in your meadow. You are not separate from it. You are part of it, holding still a moment while it carries on.

**G4 · Contact** *(new — outward-directed alternative to G1, per §1.4)*
> Pick a spot on the floor and stay with it. Not to concentrate. Just somewhere to rest your eyes while the rest of you works.

## Outdoor cardio — run, walk, cycle

**G5 · Contact**
> Listen to your feet. Not the pace of them — the sound. Then your breathing underneath it. Two rhythms, not quite matching.

**G6 · Place**
> What is the air doing to your skin right now? Cold on your face, warm on your arms, moving or still. You are outside, and your body already knows it.

**G7 · Place**
> See how far you can hear. Birds, wind, a road somewhere. Something further off than you first noticed.

**G8 · Beyond** *(rewritten — v1's version instructed empathic distress. §1.5)*
> Someone is walking home ahead of you. You will never know what kind of day they have had. You do not need to. You can just wish them a good evening and keep going.

**G9 · Beyond**
> Somebody is out in their garden. Somebody else has just got in from work. All of that carries on around you while you move through it, and none of it is about you. There is something restful in that.

## Loaded strength — squat, deadlift, press, row

> ⚠️ **Between sets only.** Never during a working set — §1.1. v1 placed these during the lift, which the evidence does not support.

**G10 · Contact** *(rest gap)*
> While you get your breath — feel your feet. All of it. Heels, outside edge, the ball of each foot. The floor pushed back exactly as hard as you pushed down.

**G11 · Place** *(rest gap)*
> Whatever is under you now, there is ground under that. The same ground either way, holding you up without being asked.

## Floor work — stretches, mobility, mat exercises

**G12 · Contact**
> Let something go heavy. Whatever part of you is touching the floor, stop holding it up. The floor has it.

**G13 · Place**
> Warm ground after a day of sun. That is what you are lying on. Give it a second and see whether your body half believes it.

## Seated work

**G14 · Contact**
> Notice where the chair is holding you. Backs of the legs, base of the spine. You are supported right now without doing anything about it.

**G15 · Place** *(new)*
> Find the furthest thing you can see from where you are sitting. A doorway, a tree, a rooftop. Let your eyes go all the way out there for a moment.

## Balance work

> ⚠️ Between sets only — attention shift while balancing is a fall risk.

**G16 · Contact** *(rest gap)*
> Those small corrections you were making — the tiny adjustments at your ankle. Nobody taught you those. Your body was doing arithmetic you never learn consciously.

## Indoor cardio machines — treadmill, bike, rower

> v1's G15 reached for the *Beyond* step with no sensory step beneath it and landed abruptly. **Cut.** Replaced with two that work with the setting rather than apologising for it.

**G17 · Contact** *(new)*
> No weather in here, no view. So: your breath and the machine. See if you can hear one over the other.

**G18 · Place** *(new)*
> This is a room built for going nowhere. Somewhere out there it is raining, or not. Picture the weather you are missing, and let this be the warm dry version.

## Self-directed *Beyond* — any family

> Per §1.5 and Neff (2003), pointing the *Beyond* step at yourself is a legitimate version, not a lesser one. **Also the safest first `Beyond` for someone new**, since it asks nothing of anyone else.

**G19 · Beyond**
> Think of yourself on a harder day than this one. Not to compare. Just to notice that the person who turns up on those days is the same one here now.

**G20 · Beyond**
> Whatever you would say to somebody else doing this — try aiming it at yourself for a second. Most people find that harder. It is worth noticing that it is harder.

# Part 4 — What I would change if you disagree with nothing

Three honest weaknesses, so you are not looking for them.

**G20 comes closest to instruction.** *"It is worth noticing that it is harder"* tells the person what to conclude. Neff's framing supports the content, but the last sentence does the noticing for them. Probably cut the final line.

**G18's "let this be the warm dry version" is the most writerly line here**, and writerly is a risk in a voice built on plainness. It may be charming or it may be the one line that sounds like an app trying.

**The imagined-nature evidence is the weakest link in Part 1.** §1.2's real-nature findings are solid; the extension to *imagined* nature — which G2, G3, G13 and G18 all depend on — rests on a smaller literature and a citation I have flagged as less certain. If that evidence does not hold up, the *Place* step still works as attention-direction, but it loses the restoration claim.

# Part 5 — Open questions

1. **Depth gating** — confirm the session thresholds, or make depth a preference rather than a progression?
2. **Rest-gap delivery** — does the rest gap in `gym-programme.js` and `workout.js` have somewhere to put one, or is that a build?
3. **The attentional-focus trade-off** — Philosophy page, or kept internal? It is a genuinely interesting thing to be honest about publicly: *most fitness apps would tell you focusing on the muscle makes you stronger; the evidence says it makes you bigger and slightly worse at the lift, so we only ask when the lift is not the point.*
4. **Interaction with In Step** — In Step is four movements, sixteen scenarios, Personal tier, deliberate. Grounding moments are seconds long, free, and in-exercise. Complementary or confusing?
5. **The opt-out** — global setting, or per-moment dismissal that is simply remembered?
